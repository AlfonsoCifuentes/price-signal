The question

Is a product priced differently, or is it a different product?

A price list is a list of assumptions.

A catalogue looks simple: a series, a title, a price. Behind each title sit a product type, a level, an edition, a pack, an answer key, an access licence and sometimes a book split into two parts.

Average across all that and you get a confident wrong answer. The pooled mean here is €44.04, a number that describes no product at all: a workbook averages €24.72, a student book €60.51.

The material

Every name and price below was invented by a seeded generator. Same seed, same file, byte for byte.

Five columns in. Nineteen out.

The raw file has 362 rows across 12 invented series, in two level systems: CEFR for adults and teens, grades for primary. Each row carries only five fields.

Enrichment adds fourteen attributes that the comparison needs. Some can be read from the title; others, like edition and access duration, never appear there and have to come from metadata. That difference matters later.

The method

Four steps, in this order. Skipping the first is how analysis quietly destroys evidence.

Hold the product still. Then look at the price.

  1. Keep the source.

    The raw file is never edited. Every correction lives in a separate reporting view that can be switched off, so any number can be traced back to the record it came from.

  2. Recover the structure.

    A rule-based parser reads titles only. It matched the reference labels on level, pack, answer key, book part, audio and digital access for all 362 rows. On product type it abstained twice, on “TB” and “Gleebook”, rather than guess. The rules were written for this catalogue’s own title patterns, so that score checks the rules; it is not a benchmark for real data.

    Not recoverable from titles: edition · access duration · extra resources · segment

  3. Compare like with like.

    Products are grouped on ten attributes: series, type, role, part, pack, answer key, both digital rights, resources and edition. Only the level may change. That yields 68 groups, of which 60 span two or more levels.

  4. Separate fact from guess.

    A price difference is a question for the commercial team, not a verdict. Each exception is classified, explained and left in the data, so the reader can disagree.

The exceptions

The interesting errors don’t show where a record belongs. They show where it landed.

Six exceptions. Four Starter prices, two filing errors.

Of 60 comparable groups, 54 hold one price at every level. In four of the six that don’t, only the Starter level is cheaper, a plausible entry-level price. The other two aren’t prices at all: each contains a record from another series, filed under the wrong name.

Matched comparisons

Each comparison below holds series and level fixed, and checks edition labels. Nothing is compared to the catalogue average.

Three comparisons that hold up.

Split volumes

Part A + Part B costs more than the complete book.

Little Lanterns: €62.00 for two parts against €54.49 for one volume, +13.8%. Wildflower Words: +13.9%. Same at every level, including the two Little Lanterns levels where the parts and the complete volume carry different edition labels.

Entry level

Starter is cheaper in two series, and only there.

Mosaic English and Silverline Skills price Starter student books −€8.00 and workbooks −€4.00 below their other levels (−14.0% to −19.5%). The other ten series don’t. Strategy is plausible; list prices alone can’t confirm it.

Answer keys

With key or without, the price is the same.

24 of 24 workbook pairs, matched on series, level and edition, share one price. Parity only means something because the match is strict.

Quality register

Stet: let it stand. Query: ask the owner. Normalise: alias in reporting only.

Ten things that look like facts.

Each issue was planted in the generator on purpose, then found, measured and logged. None was silently fixed.

Series field contradicts the title2 rowsQuery

Rows 20 and 155 carry another series’ name. They create two of the six price “exceptions”. The source keeps the recorded value; the reporting view re-files them by title prefix.

Two spellings of one edition3 + 3 rowsNormalise

“Second Edition” and “2nd Edition” appear on six Little Lanterns rows. Alias them for reporting. Because edition is part of the comparison key, these rows sit outside every cross-level group. They are six of the 8 single-level groups; the other two are the TB pack and the Gleebook resource, each the only product of its kind.

“Gleebook” is not an eBook1 rowStet

A printed resource whose name contains the letters “ebook”. A substring rule grants it digital access; a word-boundary rule doesn’t. The record is correct, so it stands. The parser was the problem.

One pack, two names6 vs 24 rowsQuery

Mosaic English calls its study pack “+ Online Access”; every other series says “with Digital”. Probably the same thing, but merging them is a business decision, not a parsing one.

Primary book filed as Secondary1 rowQuery

Row 61, Fable Road Level 1 Pupil Book: segment Primary, sub-segment Secondary. Every other Fable Road row says Primary. A hierarchy error that would skew any segment report.

The “TB” outlier€108.49Investigate

The only price above the upper IQR fence (about €100). “TB” could mean teacher’s book or something else entirely, so its type stays unresolved. Flag it; don’t delete it to tidy a chart.

Access duration, mostly unstated6 of 72Stet

Six digital workbooks state 18 months; sixty-six say nothing. Blank means unknown, not unlimited. Treating the gap as “no limit” would invent a product feature.

Part A + B premium12 of 12 levelsInvestigate

Buying both halves costs about 14% more than the complete volume, at every level of both split series. At two Little Lanterns levels the parts and the volume carry different edition labels; the premium is the same either way. Possibly intended; worth confirming before anyone calls it an error.

Starter priced below other levels4 groupsInvestigate

Two series, student book and workbook only. Consistent enough to look deliberate. The data shows the pattern; the reason has to come from a person.

Answer-key parity24 of 24 pairsStet

With-key and without-key workbooks always share a price when matched on series, level and edition. A clean result, logged because a looser match would have hidden it.

Limits

Said plainly, so nobody has to infer it.

What a list price can’t tell you.

Not in the data

  • Sales
  • Costs
  • Margins
  • Discounts
  • Elasticity

So there is no recommended price here, and no claim about profitability. What the analysis can do is hand a commercial team four precise questions:

  1. Is the Starter discount in Mosaic English and Silverline Skills deliberate?
  2. Who owns series metadata, and how did rows 20 and 155 get filed under the wrong name?
  3. Is the premium of about 14% for buying a book in two parts intended?
  4. What is “TB”, and does €108.49 belong in the same catalogue?
